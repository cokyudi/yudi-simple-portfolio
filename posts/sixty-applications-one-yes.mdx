## It's Over

On September 25th I accepted an offer. I start in October.

A month ago I wrote [a post from day twenty-two](/blog/finding-a-software-engineer-job-in-japan) and ended it with "I do not know how this one ends yet." Now I know, so this is the other half: the full numbers, and what they say that I couldn't see from the middle.

This was my second search this year. In May I wrote about [joining a startup](/blog/new-chapter-startup) after five months of looking. It turned out not to be the right fit, and by August I was searching again.

## The Whole Funnel

Here's the search in one line: **60 applications → 18 interviews → 4 offers → 1 yes.**

<Figure
  src="/images/blog/job-search-sankey.webp"
  alt="Sankey diagram of 60 applications split by source (agency/referral 21, inbound scout 13, direct application 26) flowing into interviewed 18, screened out 25, no reply 12 and withdrew 5, then down to 4 offers: 1 accepted, 3 declined"
  caption="Every application from this search, from where it started to where it ended."
/>

The diagram shows every thread I tracked. A few things stood out once I put it all in one picture.

**Direct applications almost never became conversations.** I sent 26 cold applications through company portals. Nearly all of them ended at document screening or got no reply at all. Only a thin green line makes it to the interview column.

**Agencies and scouts did almost all the work.** Referrals and inbound scouts made up 34 of the 60, and they produced almost every interview and every offer. When someone who knows the company carries your résumé to a person, you skip the keyword filter. In August I thought this was a pattern. Now I'm sure of it.

**I said no more than I expected to.** Four offers, three declined. Some of that was timing, and some was that my criteria changed partway through (more on that below). Nine rejections after interviews hurt less than I thought they would, because I was also turning things down.

## Most of the Effort Went in Early

Fifty of the sixty applications went out in the first four weeks. After that it dropped to a trickle, because I had more interviews than I could prepare for properly.

The funny part is that the offer I accepted wasn't one of the early ones. It opened on August 27th, after my busiest weeks were over, and went from first contact to signed in 29 days: first interview, on-site, offer, done. All that volume in August didn't produce the result directly. What it produced was practice, so by the time the right conversation showed up I was sharp enough not to waste it.

## The Question I Stopped Asking

At the start I was optimizing for growth: the hardest problems, the strongest engineers to learn from, the most impressive name. About five weeks in I realized I was interviewing for a version of the job I didn't actually want.

What I wanted, once I was honest about it, was simpler: work I'd still enjoy in a year, a sane balance with the rest of my life, and a stable income. That changed which offers looked good. A couple of processes that looked like my best chances on paper stopped fitting, and I withdrew from them. It felt like throwing away progress. Looking back, it was the most useful decision of the whole search.

If you're in the middle of this: check what you're optimizing for about halfway through. It may have changed without you noticing.

## Things I'd Tell Myself on Day One

- **Talk to people instead of mass-applying.** One recruiter who actually knows the company is worth ten cold applications.
- **Track everything from the first day.** Every number in this post comes from notes I kept as I went. Without them I would have remembered it as "lots of rejections," and that wouldn't have been true.
- **Live coding is its own skill.** I froze on an algorithm question in one round and did well on an AI-assisted coding test in another. Same engineer, different format. Practise the format, not just the fundamentals.
- **A rejection is information, not a verdict.** Twenty-five screen-outs mostly told me which channels to drop, not how good I am.
